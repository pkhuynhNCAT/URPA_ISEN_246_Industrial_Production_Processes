# Maker Academy

## A friendly guide to learning manufacturing, one small step at a time

**For NCAT ISEN 246 — Industrial Production Processes**

Maker Academy is a learning game about making useful products from plastic and metal. You might heat a plastic strip, make a clip, inflate a bottle, follow metal into a mold, investigate a defect, or compare the cost of two production plans.

You do not need to know the machines before you begin. A built-in guide named **Milo** tells you what to do, what to watch, and what the result means. You can use hints, repeat an experiment, and take your time.

The app supports the **Week 2 plastics** and **Week 4 casting** materials supplied for ISEN 246. It is a practice companion for those topics. It does not cover every topic in the entire course.

**The first thing to do:** open **Maker_Academy.html** in a browser and press **Show me how to play**. You can start learning without reading this whole guide.

## 2. Your first experiment

The opening walkthrough teaches you how to use the app by helping you make a plastic clip. It has only three small steps.

### Step 1: heat the plastic

Press **Heat the plastic**. Watch the temperature indicator rise and the strip bend.

When the movement ends, read the box called **What just happened**. It explains that this kind of plastic becomes easier to shape when heated.

Press **I see it — continue** when you are ready. The app waits for you.

### Step 2: shape it and cool it

Press **Shape and cool my clip**. Watch the strip take a new shape.

Read the result: cooling helps the plastic become firm and hold its shape. Then press **I see it — continue**.

### Step 3: explain what helped

Answer the small question about what made the plastic easier to reshape. Think about the change you saw when it was heated.

If your answer is incorrect, the app gives a clue. Choose again. You do not lose a life or points.

Then press **Start Lesson 1 with my guide**.

**The idea to remember:** heating can soften a suitable thermoplastic; cooling helps it hold the new shape. The first lesson then compares that behavior with a cured thermoset, which has permanent links between its chains.

## 3. How a lesson works

Every lesson follows the same three phases. You can see the current phase near the lesson title. A **Next** line names the useful action.

| Phase                         | Your job                                                                               | What you learn                        |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **1 · Make it with me**       | Press the named action button. Watch one change. Read the result. Continue when ready. | How the process works.                |
| **2 · Try a small challenge** | Choose a card or change a setting. Run or check your idea. Read the explanation.       | How to make a manufacturing decision. |
| **3 · Remember the idea**     | Answer two short questions. Use hints if you need them.                                | Why the decision makes sense.         |

### During a demonstration

Milo gives one short instruction, such as **Fill the mold**. The **Look for** sentence tells you where to look. Press the action button and follow the movement.

The demonstration stops at its result. Read **What just happened** before continuing. You can replay the movement. After the first demonstration step, a **Previous small step** button lets you return to an earlier demonstration.

### During a challenge

For a card-based challenge:

1. Read the situation and its clue.
2. Choose one card. A selected card gets a visible check mark.
3. A Run button appears below the cards. Its wording fits the task, such as **Heat my sample** or **Use this inspection method**.
4. Press that button to test your idea.
5. Read the feedback. If necessary, choose another card and run it again.
6. After a successful experiment, read the explanation and continue.

**Choosing a card is only the first action. You still need to run the experiment.**

Some challenges use a slider or a number box instead. The guide tells you which control to use and which Check button to press.

Some questions ask you to predict what a material will do. The experiment shows that material’s behavior even if your prediction was incorrect. Other questions ask you to identify a process, put a step in order, or choose evidence. Their diagrams help explain the idea; they do not all represent adjustable physical models.

### During the two understanding questions

Choose the answer that best fits the question. Read the explanation after answering. Pay attention to words such as **can**, **cannot**, and **unsuitable**: a question may ask you to identify a method that will not work.

A lesson badge appears after the practice and both understanding questions are complete. The badge screen gives you one idea to take back to class.

| **Another small hint**         | Gives more support. The third level supplies an explicit choice or action.                      |
| **Show me what to click**      | Highlights and focuses the useful control. For a choice question, it can show the correct card. |

## 6. The eleven lessons

The course path contains **31 guided demonstration steps, 53 practice rounds, and 22 understanding questions**, in addition to the beginner walkthrough. These counts describe learning activities; they do not mean there are 53 separate engineering simulations.

| Lesson                                  | What you do                                                                                    | The idea to take away                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Meet the materials**               | Heat a thermoplastic, compare a cured thermoset, and identify near net shape.                  | Different plastics behave differently when reheated. A nearly finished shape still needs appropriate checks. |
| **2. Make a plastic clip**              | Follow melting, filling, cooling, and release. Investigate a soft part and an incomplete fill. | Filling and cooling solve different problems. Rushing a cycle can create rejected parts.                     |
| **3. Find the right process**           | Match six customer orders to manufacturing processes.                                          | The starting material and required shape are useful clues.                                                   |
| **4. Build a hollow tank**              | Follow powder as it tumbles, sticks, joins, becomes a continuous wall, and cools.              | Rotational molding builds the wall while leaving a hollow middle.                                            |
| **5. Turn metal into a part**           | Follow the casting workflow and the path into a mold.                                          | Prepare, melt, pour, solidify, remove, finish, and inspect. A vent and a riser have different jobs.          |
| **6. What can we use again?**           | Sort eight casting processes by pattern and mold reuse.                                        | A reusable pattern does not make an individual sand mold reusable.                                           |
| **7. Rescue a casting**                 | Investigate gas pores, shrinkage, an unfilled corner, an unjoined seam, and metal penetration. | Identify the cause before changing the process for the next pour.                                            |
| **8. Make the size come out right**     | Adjust a pattern length and compare straight walls with a gentle taper.                        | Allowance helps the final size; draft helps release.                                                         |
| **9. Be a quality detective**           | Choose evidence for size, surface flaws, hidden flaws, strength, and alloy composition.        | The inspection method must answer the actual question.                                                       |
| **10. Find the better production cost** | Change order size, compare total costs, and find where the totals are equal.                   | Starting costs and cost per part matter differently at different quantities.                                 |
| **11. Your factory challenge**          | Combine the material, process, defect, inspection, and cost ideas.                             | Use a specific requirement or clue to explain your decision.                                                 |

The six plastic processes are **injection molding, blow molding, extrusion, thermoforming, compression molding, and rotational molding**. Their differences are also explained in the field guide.

## 7. Three examples you can follow

### Example A: improve a plastic clip

Open **Lesson 2: Make a plastic clip** and complete the four guided actions: heat, fill, cool, and release.

In the first challenge, the mold filled, but the part was still too soft when it came out. The useful clue is **too soft after filling**. Choose **Give the part enough cooling**, then press **Make a clip with this setting**.

Watch the cycle and read the explanation. Enough cooling helps the clip hold its shape in this model. Use **Before** and **After** to compare the distorted part with the improved result.

In the next challenge, the cavity is not completely filled. The picture marks an **Unfilled area**. Choose **Investigate melt readiness and filling**, then press **Test the filling change**. Filling the cavity addresses the missing material; painting the part would not supply it.

The two cases teach different problems: **a filled part can be too soft to remove, while a short shot never filled the whole shape.**

### Example B: make a part finish at 100 mm

Open **Lesson 8: Make the size come out right**. Complete the demonstrations, then use the pattern-length slider or number box.

The exercise uses a simple rule: the cooled part is **2% shorter than the pattern**. This number is an assumption for learning.

Before pressing Check, try these values:

| Pattern length | Calculated cooled length | What to notice                                                       |
| -------------: | -----------------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|      100.00 mm |                 98.00 mm | Too short. Starting at the target size does not allow for shrinkage. |
|      102.04 mm |          About 100.00 mm | Inside the target range.                                             |
|      104.00 mm |                101.92 mm | Too long. A bigger allowance is not always better.                   |

For a successful trial, enter **102.04** and press **Check my size**. Watch the lower bar shorten as it cools. The result diagram keeps your chosen pattern length; it does not return to the starting value.

The acceptable cooled range is **99.90 to 100.10 mm**. That is what **100.00 ± 0.10 mm** means.

If you want to see the calculation:

```text
Cooled length = pattern length × 0.98
Pattern length for a 100 mm target = 100 ÷ 0.98
                                 ≈ 102.04 mm
```

The next question introduces **draft**, a slight taper that helps release. Making the pattern larger and adding a taper solve different problems.

### Example C: choose a production plan

Open **Lesson 10: Find the better production cost**. The app compares two invented plans that are both assumed to meet the design requirements.

- **Route A:** pay $2,000 to start, then $8 for every part.
- **Route B:** pay $20,000 to start, then $2 for every part.

Press **Small order** and **Large order**, or move the quantity slider. Read the dollar totals as well as the bars. The bars rescale to fit the drawing, so compare the two bars within the current view.

| Number of parts | Route A total | Route B total | Lower total |
| --------------: | ------------: | ------------: | ----------- |
|             100 |        $2,800 |       $20,200 | A           |
|           3,000 |       $26,000 |       $26,000 | Equal       |
|          10,000 |       $82,000 |       $40,000 | B           |

For a small order, B has not made enough parts to recover its much larger starting cost. For a large order, its saving on each extra part adds up.

**Break-even** means the totals are equal. B starts $18,000 higher and saves $6 per part, so divide **18,000 by 6**. The answer is **3,000 parts**.

These are teaching costs, not supplier quotations. First check whether a process can make the required part; then compare its cost.

## 8. How to read and control the simulations

### Look for one change

Read the **Look for** sentence before pressing Run. It might ask you to follow a stream, watch a mold open, or compare a changing number.

The app includes moving pellets and material paths, a bending strip, a changing clip shape, an inflating bottle, a growing tube, a forming sheet, a closing press, a rotating mold, rising metal, moving gas bubbles, a releasing part, and an inspection reveal. The cost and pattern activities also calculate values from your controls.

Orange often represents warm or flowing material. Blue or gray may represent a cooled part, a tool, or another labeled feature. The labels and written explanations tell you what each feature means; color alone is not the answer.

A **cutaway** is a drawing that hides part of a machine or mold so you can see inside. For example, the blow-molding view exposes the bottle even though the real mold is closed during inflation. The chain drawings are enlarged explanations of material structure, not the visible surface of a plastic part.

### Take more time when you need it

Use **Pause movement** to freeze an experiment. Use **Enlarge** to look closely; an active experiment pauses while you inspect the larger view. Closing it leaves the experiment paused so you can choose when to resume.

Use **See the final frame** if you want the result immediately. Use **Pause motion** if you prefer to learn with still results and written explanations. The app also respects a reduced-motion preference when it first creates a new progress record.

The animation progress bar shows the illustration’s progress. It is not a countdown, a score, or a measurement of real manufacturing time.

### What a casting improvement means

The defect activities change the **next pour**. For example, improved venting can help gas escape before the next casting becomes solid. A feeding riser can supply liquid metal during solidification.

Adding a vent or riser afterward does not repair pores or shrinkage cavities in an already-solid casting. The app’s short animations simplify the prevention mechanism.

### What the models can and cannot tell you

The app teaches concepts. Its drawings are simplified and not to scale. It does not calculate real temperatures, pressure, flow fields, stresses, tooling design, or defect probability. It is not a machine-control system or a real operating procedure.

A successful illustrated result means the choice fits the stated teaching example. Real production needs suitable materials, process design, qualified procedures, and checks against actual requirements.

### The field-guide pictures work differently

Under **Field guide → Plastic processes**, choose **Watch the diagram**. This opens a manual reference with a named step, a picture, and a short explanation.

Use **Next**, **Previous step**, or **Start over**. The reference stops at the last step instead of cycling through an extra blank stage. The main lessons contain the moving experiments.

## 9. Keep track of what you learn

### Badges and practice XP

Completing a lesson earns one badge and **100 practice XP**. There are 11 badges and a maximum of **1,100 XP**. Repeating a completed lesson does not add another copy of its badge or more points.

There are no lost lives, countdowns, required streaks, or leaderboards. A badge is a record of practice in this browser, not a verified course grade.

### Explain the idea in your own words

The badge screen gives a short **Take this idea back to class** prompt. Try answering it aloud to a partner, an instructor, or yourself.

You can also open **Save my idea in one sentence (optional)** and type your explanation. One sentence is enough. For example:

> Cooling helped my thermoplastic clip keep its shape before it left the mold.

Each change to your text saves automatically in the same browser when saving is available. There is no separate Save button. It stays when you replay the lesson and appears in the learning report. The app does not grade it or send it to an AI service. Avoid entering private personal information.

### Download a learning report

Open **My badges**, then choose **Download learning report**. The app creates a text file with your badge total, lesson learning statements, completed practice counts, understanding-check progress, source references, and any explanations you wrote.

You can keep the file or give it to your instructor as requested. The app does not send it automatically. The report is a readable record; it cannot be imported to restore your progress.

You can also use **Print my passport** on the badges page to open the browser’s print dialog for that overview. Printing depends on the browser and device.

The report can show an earned badge alongside a fresh, unfinished replay attempt. This is expected: the old badge remains while the new practice attempt is tracked separately.

## 10. Stop now and continue later

When your browser allows local saving, the app remembers your current lesson and small step. It also remembers accepted answers, selected cards, hints, numerical settings, completed demonstrations, badges, written explanations, and your motion preference.

After finishing the beginner walkthrough, the welcome screen offers **Continue my learning**. You can also choose a lesson from **My lessons**.

Keep using the same browser, profile, and file location or website address. Renaming or moving a local HTML file, clearing browser data, changing profiles, or using private browsing may affect saved progress. Behavior varies between browsers.

An unfinished opening walkthrough restarts at its first step. A partly played animation is not saved frame by frame. If you leave an experiment before it finishes, that unfinished action is not marked complete.

### If saving is unavailable

The app can still run for the current session. The footer tells you that progress cannot be saved. Download a learning report before closing if you want a record.

### Starting over

**My badges → Reset progress** asks for confirmation before clearing this app’s record in the current browser. That includes badges, lesson progress, and written explanations. Choose **Keep my progress** to cancel.

Students who share the same browser profile and file location may also share the same app record. Use separate profiles or a confirmed reset when separate practice records are needed.

## 11. Using a keyboard, phone, or tablet

You can use **Tab** to move between controls and **Enter** or **Space** to activate a focused button. Range sliders support the browser’s arrow-key controls. The pattern-size activity also has a number box, so dragging is not required. Enter can submit the pattern and break-even number entries.

The interface has visible focus outlines, labeled controls, written result explanations, and feedback announcements. Browser zoom can make the interface larger.

On a narrow screen, the picture and Milo’s instructions stack vertically. The app brings the moving picture into view when an experiment starts and brings the explanation into view when it finishes. You may need to scroll between them during ordinary reading.

Use **Enlarge** when diagram labels are small. On a phone, scroll sideways inside the enlarged view to see the full drawing.

There is no required sound. Turning off movement does not remove the explanations or the ability to complete the course. Screen-reader and device-specific behavior still need checking on the devices used in your class.

## 13. A simple plan for instructors

### Before class

Open the app on the actual student devices. Complete the beginner walkthrough and one lesson. Check that the buttons, diagrams, saving, and report download work in the way you plan to distribute the file.

Explain the goal in one sentence:

> We will make a small manufacturing decision, watch its result, and explain the idea behind it.

The HTML can be distributed directly or served from a static website. The README does not need to be uploaded with it for the app to run. No backend or account system is required. The delivered files do not themselves publish a website or modify the original Drive folder.

### A first class activity

1. Complete the three-step opening walkthrough together.
2. Ask students to describe what changed during heating and cooling.
3. Begin Lesson 1. Model using a hint so students know help is allowed.
4. Let students try a challenge independently or with a partner.
5. End with one explanation in the student’s own words. Save it in the optional note or ask students to say it aloud.

Starting with one lesson is enough. Add Lesson 2 when students are comfortable with the choose-and-run interaction.

### Later sessions

| Session focus                      | Suggested lessons |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Plastic process choices            | 3–4               |
| Casting steps and reusable tooling | 5–6               |
| Defect investigation               | 7                 |
| Size planning and inspection       | 8–9               |
| Cost and combined decisions        | 10–11             |

Students can revisit any lesson. These groupings are suggestions, not fixed timings.

### Check learning beyond badges

Useful questions include:

- What changed in the picture?
- Which clue helped you decide?
- Why would the other choice fail to address the problem?
- Can you explain the same idea using a different product?

For a student who needs more time, ask for one observation before asking for the technical term. For example, start with “the plastic became easier to bend,” then connect that observation to “thermoplastic.”

A report can support discussion, but XP should not rank speed or ability. Look for the student’s explanation and ability to use the idea in another example. Learning gains and student enjoyment have not been measured in a classroom trial of this app.

## 14. Privacy and offline use

The app has no accounts, ads, analytics, external fonts, external scripts, background network requests, or live AI calls. Student choices and written notes stay in the current browser’s local record when saving is available.

Source links open outside pages only when clicked. Those pages require Internet access and may require permission. Their privacy rules belong to the external service.

The downloadable learning report contains the local practice information and written explanations. Students decide whether to share it. The app does not email, upload, or submit reports automatically.

### A few important distinctions

- Suitable thermoplastics can often be reprocessed by heating; contamination, degradation, and recycling facilities still matter. Cured thermosets do not ordinarily remelt, although other recovery methods may exist.
- Polyester and polyurethane are broad families. Their names alone do not classify every formulation as thermoplastic or thermoset.
- Compression molding is not universally the strongest or most durable route. Material, reinforcement, geometry, and processing determine performance.
- High-pressure die casting is not automatically pore-free.
- Misrun and cold shut are distinguished, as are sand inclusion and metal penetration.
- Dye penetrant finds openings at a suitable surface, not sealed internal voids. This distinction was checked against [ASNT’s liquid penetrant guidance](https://www.asnt.org/what-is-nondestructive-testing/methods/liquid-penetrant-testing).
- Internal radiographic imaging and chemical-composition analysis answer different questions, even when a composition method also uses X-rays.
- The slides’ cost, pressure, and cycle ranges are illustrative. They are not current quotations or universal limits. Different rotational-cycle ranges in the slides are not treated as conflicting operating instructions.
- A product may have more than one plausible manufacturing route. The practice questions specify clues that make a particular choice fit the stated brief.

### Numerical assumptions

The pattern exercise defines shrinkage as **2% of the pattern length** and accepts a cooled length of **100.00 ± 0.10 mm**. It uses the unrounded result to check the target. A 102.04 mm pattern calculates to 99.9992 mm, displayed as 100.00 mm. These values are teaching assumptions, not a prescription for a particular alloy.

The cost exercise assumes both routes meet the same design requirements. Its totals are `2000 + 8 × quantity` and `20000 + 2 × quantity`. It omits real factors such as scrap, capacity, maintenance, financing, and lead time. A lower illustrated total does not make a route suitable for every product.

The app uses newly drawn schematics and paraphrased explanations. It is a teaching companion and does not imply institutional endorsement.
